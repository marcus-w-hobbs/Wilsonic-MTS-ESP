Fifteen questions about Erv Wilson, with things to play

Stephen Weigel, you sent fifteen questions for the Wilsember episode and I could not be on the call, so I built you this page. Each question has a short answer from me, and where the idea is something you can see or hear I put a small instrument under it so you can try it yourself. I worked beside Erv from 1995 to 2005, turning his drawings into software while he drew the next one, and for the last year I have been going back through his papers with a computer.

You have to connect ideas to sources when you take them to your listeners, so every claim on this page carries one of three labels, and the full list of sources is at the bottom.

Question 1 of 15

What makes Erv Wilson a unique force in the microtonal space?

I think it is that he would not choose between melody and harmony. A scale that is good for melody wants a few step sizes and a clear stepwise shape, and a scale that is good for harmony wants chords whose tones reinforce each other, and most systems give you one of those and ask you to live without the other. Erv spent his life looking for structures that give you both at once. He worked by hand and by ear and he recorded what he found as drawings, because that is how he thought.

The test he kept coming back to was the triad. Take two notes and put a third note at their arithmetic mean and you have the major kind of triad, the 4:5:6 kind. Both gaps between the notes are then the same number of hertz, so the difference tones land together on one pitch and the chord locks. Put the third note at the harmonic mean and you have the minor kind, 10:12:15, where the three notes share an overtone. Erv called these proportional and subcontrary triads, and in Wilsonic the analysis that counts them in every scale still uses his names.

What I did not expect, when I measured it on myself, was how forgiving the ear is. In blind listening last autumn, under the strictest condition I tried, a major third stopped sounding locked for me once it was about 8.43 cents flat or 10.0 cents sharp. That is one listener and one timbre, so I would not build a theory on it yet.

The line is frequency in hertz, which is where difference tones live. Move the mistuning slider and watch the two small difference tones separate, then listen for the beating.

Question 2 of 15

Do recurrent sequences in his scale-making produce moments of symmetry? Or other types of patterns as well?

They do, and the way they get there is the part I find most interesting. Erv read the diagonals of Pascal's triangle, which he called Mt. Meru, as rules for building a sequence where every new number is the sum of two earlier ones. The ratio between neighbouring terms settles toward one number, and that number is a generator. If you stack that generator and stop at one of its natural sizes, you have a moment of symmetry. Fibonacci is the first diagonal, and his meta-pelog, meta-slendro and meta-meantone come from the diagonals after it.

So in the long run the answer is yes. In the early terms, before the ratio has settled, you get a different pattern, and as a musician I find it more useful. Because each new term is the sum of two earlier terms, half of the new term is their arithmetic mean, so the three of them make an exact proportional triad in whole numbers. The start of the sequence gives you harmony and the far end gives you the melodic pattern, and in between the scale slides from one to the other.

When I checked this by machine in October the exact-triad rule held for every recurrence whose coefficients are powers of two, which covers the ones Erv wrote down that I tested. A second rule I had registered, about how fast the harmony gives way to the chain, failed on one case in twelve, and I have left it marked as refuted. I have not finished listening to the scales this made.

Try it: watch a recurrence settle

Set the window to one of the generator's own sizes, then slide the start to the right. Early windows have several step sizes and exact triads. Late windows have two step sizes, which is the moment of symmetry.

Question 3 of 15

How can one use the scale tree?

I use it as a map of generators. Every fraction on the tree is a generator measured as a fraction of the octave, and its denominator is the number of notes you have when the chain closes. When you walk down the tree toward a generator, the denominators you pass are the sizes at which that generator gives a moment of symmetry, so one path is a list of scales that live inside each other. A path that stops gives an equal temperament. A path that turns left, right, left, right forever settles on what Erv called a golden generator, where the large and small steps approach the same proportion at every size. Mathematicians know the same tree as the Stern-Brocot tree and those endless zigzag numbers as noble numbers.

Last October I transcribed the bottom row of Erv's 1994 Scale-Tree sheet, which is 32 decimals, and found that one rule reproduces all 32 to ten places: take each pair of neighbouring fractions a/b and c/d at the fifth row and write (a + φc)/(b + φd). His sheet shows the numbers and leaves the rule for the reader to find.

I expected the golden generators to explain why Erv's favourite generators sound so harmonic, and the measurement disappointed me. His generators do sit within a fraction of a cent of golden ones, but golden numbers are so dense that a random generator does too. Against a fair comparison I never found more than 6 of his 17 in the closest tenth, where chance gives 1.7, and the silver family fits them as well as the golden one. My reading today is that the zigzag is about melody, meaning how the scales nest, and that harmony comes from a separate condition. I think Erv chose the golden number inside a range that already sounded good to him, and that last sentence is a guess.

My favourite generators so far come from Erv's papers on choosing a moment of symmetry for its proportional and subcontrary triads. As fractions of the octave they are 0.292787, 0.436386, 0.415227, 0.328173 and 0.238186. I made music with them on my first album, From On High, which came out in 1998 under the name Marcus Satellite, and all five ship as presets in Wilsonic. They are also in the Start from menu below, so you can walk each one down the tree and hear its scales. When I solved for them I found that four are the limit of a short recurrence, and you can try those in question 2. The generator 0.436386 comes from H[n] = H[n−3] + 2·H[n−4], 0.328173 comes from H[n] = H[n−1] + H[n−7], 0.415227 comes from H[n] = 4·H[n−8] + 8·H[n−9], and 0.238186 is the octave complement of the generator of H[n] = H[n−1] + 2·H[n−3]. I have not matched 0.292787 to a recurrence yet.

Question 4 of 15

How could we summarize the list of ideas he pioneered?

Here is the list I would read out, each with the paper to point listeners to. It is my selection, and Kraig Grady's archive holds far more than this.

  • Moments of symmetry. Stack one generator inside a period and stop where the scale has exactly two step sizes. This is the idea most of the community met first.
  • The scale tree and the golden horograms. A map of every generator and the scales nested inside it, with rings that show each size.
  • Combination product sets. Take a set of harmonic factors, multiply them k at a time, and you get a harmonic structure with no single root: the hexany, the dekanies, the eikosany and larger ones.
  • Mapping harmony onto keyboards. His generalized keyboard layouts, and D'Alessandro, where a 31-note template carries the eikosany and its neighbours.
  • Constant structures. Scales where every interval always spans the same number of steps, so a keyboard or a notation can be trusted.
  • The scales of Mt. Meru. Recurrent sequences from the diagonals of Pascal's triangle, whose limits are generators such as meta-meantone and meta-slendro.
  • Diamonds, lattices and the work on other people's music. His lattice drawings, the illustrations he made for Harry Partch's Genesis of a Music, his Marwa permutations and his latticing of raga scales.
Question 5 of 15

How do you think Wilson's ideas of being "xenharmonic" differed from Darreg's?

I am the wrong person to speak for Ivor Darreg, and I would rather hear you, Billy and Praveen on this one. The word is Darreg's, and the published accounts of what he meant by it are better than my paraphrase would be.

Erv mentioned Ivor Darreg many times. Kraig Grady and Stephen James Taylor had the context for those references and I did not, because I came into the circle as a young man of 25, so I can't tell you how their ideas differed. We all used the word xenharmonic, and Erv was a contributor to Xenharmonikon. We all struggled to find a word that covered the ideas we were making, whether that was microtonality or xenharmonic or music theory, and I think it is still unresolved.

What I can tell you is that Erv was the least ideological person I know. He kept a handwritten note to himself that read “Mr. Spock: Nothing in the universe is unnatural.” He included everything, even 12-tone equal temperament, which actually triggered me at first, and later I accepted it when I understood how inclusive his thinking was.

Question 6 of 15

What does it mean to be "Wilsonian"?

To me it means you begin with a structure and you ask it for melody and harmony together. It means you draw it, because a drawing lets you see relationships that a list of cents hides. And it means the structure is something you play your way through, moving from one subset to a neighbouring one, where another approach would fix a single scale and stay in it.

I would add one caution for your listeners. Being Wilsonian does not mean avoiding the regular temperament vocabulary. Most of Erv's objects have exact names in that language, and I have put the translations in the dictionary below because I wish I had had it in 1995.

Question 7 of 15

What does it mean for scale creation to be a living process, like a crystal or plant? Is that based in allegory only?

I do not think it is only allegory, and I can show you the part that is literal. A moment of symmetry grows by one rule applied over and over: place the next note a fixed fraction of the octave from the last one. A sunflower head grows by the same rule, with each new seed a fixed fraction of a turn from the last one. Whatever fraction you choose, the notes around the octave never have more than three different gaps between them, and at certain sizes they have exactly two. Those sizes are the moments of symmetry. For a golden generator the proportion between the large and small step settles to the golden ratio at every size, so the scale really is self-similar in the way a plant is.

The crystal side is literal too. A combination product set is a solid: the hexany is an octahedron, with a tone at each corner and a triad on each face, and you can hold a model of it in your hand.

Where I would be careful is the step after that. Sharing the arithmetic of a plant does not by itself make a scale sound good, and my own measurements so far say that the growing pattern and the harmony come from two separate conditions.

Erv would not stop talking about plants, and to a lesser degree about crystals. He was a farmer and an expert on farming, and as a mathematician he was an expert on crystals, which is where much of the work on combination product sets and lattices comes from. These ideas were absolutely fundamental to how he pictured microtonality, so for him the comparison was a working model and he used it every day.

Question 8 of 15

How can we properly remember Wilson's contributions?

By keeping his papers readable and by doing the work of deriving them again. The Wilson Archives at anaphoria.com are the authorized collection of his papers, and that is where anyone should start. Kraig Grady is the person to write to about them, and he and Terumi Narushima keep a glossary there. Terumi also wrote the book-length account of Erv's tuning systems.

What I found going back through the papers is that they show his results and leave out how he reached them. So the most useful thing I can do is work out the reasoning, check it against his numbers, and publish the code so that someone else can check me. In two posts last year I described cases where an exhaustive computer search arrived at something Erv had already drawn by hand.

Wilsonic is my part of the remembering. It is free and open source under the MIT licence, and it sends his scale designs to any synth in a DAW through MTS-ESP, so that people can play these structures and not only read about them. The listening judgments from my research are published too, under CC BY 4.0, so that others can use them or disagree with them.

Question 9 of 15

How does Wilson's influence show up in other musicians' work? What kind of correspondences did he keep and maintain?

The published record names the people, and I would send your listeners there first. Wikipedia's article on Erv lists Harry Partch, Warren Burt, Jim French, Kraig Grady, Jose Hales-Garcia, Catherine Lamb, Terumi Narushima, Paul Rapoport, Emil Richards, Greg Schiemer, Stephen James Taylor and Daniel James Wolf, and it lists me under my old stage name. Kraig's own introduction names Gary David, Daniel Wolf and Chuck Jonkey as important students and mentions the documentary about Erv. Gary David, Stephen James Taylor and I made that film, and it is called Surfing the Sonic Sky.

The papers themselves are full of correspondence, because many of his sheets were made for a particular person. Among the ones I have worked from this year are a letter to John Chalmers dated 21 August 1965, where he conjectures how the number of melodic step sizes grows with the harmonic limit, a sheet made for Kraig Grady on 15 October 1993 and a letter to Warren Burt dated 1 August 1997, both about recurrent sequences.

Erv was in good health in those years, so we met every week at Stephen James Taylor's studio. Kraig Grady lived in the US early in that period and came to the sessions until he moved to Australia, and later that circle included Terumi Narushima. Chuck Jonkey lives in LA and came often.

Erv would frequently show us letters and diagrams that he had shared with other people, but I didn't always have the context for how he had found them or who he had worked them out with. Keep in mind that I first met him in 1995, and as a tech bro I had very early access to the internet and he had none.

I actually met Erv and Stephen James Taylor through the Kyma Capybara sound design workstation. I bought a maxed-out Capybara for $8000 to explore microtonality, and one of the founders told me I needed to run, not walk, to Erv and Stephen James Taylor, who had both bought Capybaras and lived just a few miles from me. Erv never used his. I showed Stephen James Taylor how to use his, and our sessions became real-time Capybara coding sessions of Erv's research from that week. I was essentially a grad student implementing his professor's papers as he wrote them.

Question 10 of 15

Did Erv Wilson ever compose? Music making is part of the scale creation process for some people but not for others.

This is a good question for Kraig Grady and Stephen James Taylor, who knew him longer than I did. My recollection is that Erv felt his attention was best spent providing resources for composers, and that he chose that over composing himself. In our circle we had a shared picture of the ways a person can enjoy music, and it went something like this:

  • I enjoy hearing music performed by others.
  • I enjoy performing music composed by others.
  • I enjoy composing music with resources created by others. For most people that resource is 12-tone equal temperament, which is the big cultural default.
  • I enjoy creating musical and rhythmic resources for composers, performers and audiences.

Erv worked at that last level.

Question 11 of 15

Which microtonalists that you know got to speak to Erv?

The names I could confirm in print are under question 9. Kraig Grady met Erv in 1975, and Ivor Darreg is reported to have met him in the early 1960s.

The people I sat in sessions with were Erv's inner circle. Stephen James Taylor hosted us at his studio every week. Kraig Grady came until he moved to Australia, and Terumi Narushima joined that circle later. Chuck Jonkey came often from across LA. John Chalmers, who founded Xenharmonikon, visited when he could. He was in San Diego, so it was harder for him to attend, and as video conferencing improved he joined the meetups more often.

Gary David was important to Erv, though he didn't come to our weekly meetings so much. Gary is a psychologist who is really into Silvan Tomkins's affect system, and he is also a great singer. He connected with Erv in the 1960s, way before my time, and learned how to sing hexanies as jazz. After Erv passed, Gary, Stephen James Taylor and I made a documentary about him called “Surfing the Sonic Sky”, and you can watch it at https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L3gg5yrmwvs.

Question 12 of 15

What kinds of interactions did Erv's theories have with the rest of the microtonal community?

Much of it went through Xenharmonikon, the journal John Chalmers founded, where several of Erv's papers appeared between 1974 and 1989, and through letters. The 1975 paper on moments of symmetry is itself a letter to Chalmers.

The ideas keep travelling. Kraig's archive now hosts papers by Praveen Venkataramana that carry Erv's combination product sets and constant structures over to scales that repeat at the tritave, and Kraig's introduction to moments of symmetry links a calculator for them that it credits to Stiltner.

Erv collaborated with Harry Partch, and he even made a Gral keyboard mapping of Partch's tuning, which is one of the designs inside Wilsonic today. That was before my time.

Erv always had a running commentary on his collaboration with the community. In those years I was more plugged into the tech community and less into the music community, because I hated the idea of monetizing music, so I didn't pay as much attention to those matters as I wish I had.

My favorite memories are of the sessions themselves. I would implement that week's research in real time and we would hear it for the first time together, and the way Erv laughed when he heard it is one of my greatest joys and one of my best memories.

Question 13 of 15

What was the process like for his theories' adoption in the regular temperament theory space, such as horograms used in "A Middle Path" etc.?

I was not part of the conversations where that adoption happened, so I can only tell you what it looks like from Erv's side, and you know the other side better than I do. The paper you name is Paul Erlich's, and I would let Paul tell how the horograms got into it.

What I can add is that Erv's keyboard templates are vals. In D'Alessandro he laid a 31-note template over just tones from the 1·3·5·7·9·11 family. When I encoded it last year the template came out as the 31-tone patent val exactly, and the seven keys where two just tones land together are the commas in its kernel: 385/384, 2079/2048 and 121/120. Erv had no word like val or kernel. He drew the template and marked the collisions by hand.

I think the regular temperament vocabulary is the language Erv and I were missing when we worked together. I had the formal training and he had the pictures, and neither of us could translate for the other.

Going the other way, I have been asking which temperaments hold his harmonic structures. The 1·3·5·7 hexany fits inside a 22-note chain of a 193.79 cent generator with every tone within 1.37 cents, using the mapping (16, 2, 5) for 3, 5 and 7, which tempers out 2401/2400 and 3136/3125. No equal temperament below 73 notes does that. At 15 notes the best chain turned out to be a generator Erv had written down himself in July 1997, and in blind listening I preferred his 15-note chain in the harmonic passages even though its triads are 5 to 7 cents off.

One correction I had to make along the way: the claim that the 1·3·5·7·9·11 eikosany is a constant structure did not survive a machine check. Other seed sets do give constant structure eikosanies, and my search found 32 of them.

Try it: lay a template over a Wilson structure

The template is the patent val: each prime goes to its nearest step. Two tones on one degree are a collision, and the small ratio between them is a comma the template tempers out. With no collisions, every tone has its own key.

What I can show you is what I am working on now. Most music has a center, a root that feels like home, and a combination product set has none. In the hexany every tone belongs to four triads, and when you move from one triad to a neighbouring one, two voices stay and the third moves to the one tone it shares no factor with. So every chord change is one voice moving by one of three fixed intervals. With the factors 1, 3, 5 and 7 those are 21/20, 15/14 and 48/35. It feels to me like a kaleidoscope where a key feels like a grid, and I want to know what makes one chord pull toward the next when there is no home to go to. I have not answered that yet.

Question 15 of 15

Two interesting connections to explore in his work would be worldbuilding and gardening.

The gardening connection has a mathematical half and a personal half. The mathematical half is under question 7: the rule that grows a seed head is the rule that grows a moment of symmetry, and you can turn the same dial for both.

The personal half is on the public record in outline. The biography in the Wilson Archives says that Erv bred corn, and that he is sometimes credited as the first person to suggest blue corn for tortilla chips.

Erv tied his farming and his corn to scales, one hundred percent. He had bags of corn around his house, because he was breeding corn in his garden and keeping meticulous records of the results, and that is where the name “Wilson's Garden” comes from. He bred different colors of corn, he was into plant DNA, and he was always talking up corn's nutrition. Keep in mind that he was born in Mexico.

He was huge into phyllotaxis, although real plants were more of a metaphor for him. He never pointed at a cactus and said it would make a great scale, because he only used his ears to decide what a great scale was. But the structure of the physical plant world shaped how he pictured scales.

I have nothing first hand on worldbuilding, so I will leave that half to the others on the call.
